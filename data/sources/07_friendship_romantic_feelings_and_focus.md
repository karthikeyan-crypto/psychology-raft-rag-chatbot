# Friendship problems, romantic feelings, and student concentration

This source supports ordinary student questions about friendship conflicts, difficult conversations, new romantic feelings, rejection, distraction, and difficulty focusing. Such experiences do not by themselves establish a mental-health diagnosis.

## When a student says a friend is the problem

Start with empathy and invite the student to describe what happened. Useful questions include:
- "What happened with your friend?"
- "Is this a misunderstanding, an argument, or something that keeps happening?"
- "What would you most like to change about the situation?"

Offer practical, non-judgmental steps:
- If both people feel safe, choose a calm time to explain how a specific action affected you. Use "I felt..." statements and describe the event instead of attacking the person's character.
- Listen to the friend's perspective, while remembering that listening does not mean agreeing with everything.
- Think about what boundary or outcome you need. It is okay to take some space if a conversation becomes hurtful.
- If the situation includes threats, bullying, coercion, or fear for safety, involve a trusted adult, teacher, counsellor, or other appropriate support person rather than trying to manage it alone.

Do not assume who is at fault without hearing more. Do not pressure the student to maintain a friendship that feels unsafe.

## When a student says they have fallen in love or like someone

Acknowledge the feeling without teasing or judging. Ask what they want help with: understanding their feelings, talking to the person, handling uncertainty, or dealing with feelings that are not returned.

Encourage respect for both people's choices and boundaries:
- Move at a pace that feels comfortable and respectful.
- If they choose to share their feelings, be honest and accept the other person's answer without pressure.
- A person's response does not determine the student's worth.
- If the situation affects sleep, study, or daily life, suggest discussing it with a trusted person and returning to a manageable routine.

Do not promise that the other person will reciprocate, and do not encourage persistent contact after someone has said no.

## When a student cannot focus or feels distracted

Help the student choose one small, specific next step:
- Write down the task they want to complete and break it into a smaller first action.
- Put away one obvious distraction for a short study period.
- Take a brief, comfortable pause when needed, then decide on the next small task.
- Keep a realistic routine that includes sleep, meals, movement, and relaxing activities.
- If difficulty concentrating persists or interferes significantly with everyday life, encourage the student to speak with a trusted adult or qualified professional.

Do not frame these suggestions as a guaranteed treatment or imply that difficulty focusing is laziness.

## Response style

Use a warm, conversational response. Reflect what the student said, offer one or two useful next steps, and ask one gentle follow-up question. Do not provide a long generic list when the student is sharing a personal problem. Use this document as general psychoeducation, not as diagnosis or personalised treatment.
