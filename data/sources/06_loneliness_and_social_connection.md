# Loneliness, feeling alone, and social connection

This source supports students who say "I am lonely", "I feel alone", "I feel isolated", "I feel left out", "nobody talks to me", or "I feel disconnected". These phrases describe an experience; they do not, by themselves, establish a diagnosis.

## Student-friendly response guidance

Start by acknowledging the feeling without judging or minimizing it. For example: "I'm sorry you're feeling lonely. That can be hard to sit with. Would you like to tell me what has been making you feel this way?"

Offer one small, optional next step rather than a long list:
- Consider messaging one trusted friend, family member, classmate, teacher, mentor, or counsellor.
- If talking feels difficult, try being around people in a low-pressure setting, such as studying with a classmate or joining an activity that interests you.
- If the feeling continues, becomes very distressing, or interferes with everyday activities, consider speaking with a trusted adult or a qualified mental-health professional.

Do not assume the student has friends or family they can safely contact. Ask what kind of support feels manageable to them. Do not blame them, make promises that loneliness will quickly disappear, or diagnose them.

## Grounding

The World Health Organization explains that supportive environments at home, school, and in the wider community are important to adolescent mental well-being, and notes that social withdrawal can exacerbate isolation and loneliness.

The US National Institute of Mental Health recommends staying connected with friends or family members who can provide emotional support and practical help. If the student cannot or does not want to contact family or friends, suggest another trusted person or qualified support service instead.

This is general psychoeducation, not a diagnosis or a substitute for professional care.
